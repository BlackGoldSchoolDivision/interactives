# Morphology: research and teaching decisions

Reviewed October 4, 2026. These are research-informed practice activities; the games have not themselves been evaluated in a controlled intervention study.

## Findings used

**Colenbrander and colleagues (2024)** reviewed 28 studies of English morphology instruction for primary-school children. Reading and spelling outcomes were positive overall, with the clearest results for directly taught words. There was possible transfer to untaught spelling, but no clear transfer to untaught reading or reading comprehension. Heterogeneity and imprecision limited confidence in several outcomes. The review does not establish that morphology instruction is superior to other language-focused instruction.

Primary paper: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-024-09953-3

**Goodwin and Ahn (2013)** synthesized 30 studies and reported benefits for morphological knowledge, vocabulary, decoding and spelling, without significant effects on comprehension or fluency. This supports useful word-level practice without promising general comprehension gains.

Author university record and abstract: https://scholarship.miami.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-Meta-Analysis-of-Morphological-Interventions-in/991031575910102976

**Bowers, Kirby and Deacon (2010)** reviewed 22 studies and supported instruction connecting meaningful parts to literacy. Their earlier findings favoured integration with other literacy instruction. Later reviews provide more caution about transfer, age comparisons and control conditions.

Coauthor-hosted paper: https://langlabatdal.weebly.com/uploads/1/0/6/3/10631014/bowers.kirby.deacon.2010.pdf

## Our instructional choices

These choices are design inferences, not features directly proven by the reviews:

- Begin with a sentence and meaning, not a letter fragment alone.
- Use full bases in word sums: hope + ing → hoping, happy + ness → happiness, run + ing → running.
- Teach spelling changes through accurate examples, with comparison words and explicit exceptions to simplistic rules. Compare crying with tried, and playful/played with happiness.
- Explain each wrong choice and permit retries. Keep practice untimed so students can discuss their reasoning.
- Investigate genuine families and tempting letter lookalikes. Flower is not flow + er; display is not dis + play. The grain corn is distinct from the Latin element for horn in unicorn.
- Use sentence context for grammatical jobs: teacher/smaller, three paints/Noah paints, unhappy/unwrap. Show the shared past-tense job of -ed across its three pronunciations.
- Follow screen practice with speaking, reading and original sentences. Revisit words over time. Use alongside phonics and other literacy teaching.

The intended audience is roughly Grades 3–6; this is a design choice, not a formal curriculum-alignment claim or an empirically established age cutoff. Teachers can model familiar examples orally for younger learners or use the investigations with older learners who benefit from the practice.
