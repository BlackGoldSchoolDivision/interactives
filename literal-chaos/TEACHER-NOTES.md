# Literal Chaos: teaching notes

The 36 entries are original examples, with 12 flexible difficulty suggestions at each of Starter, Explorer and Expert. These are not grade placement tests. The bank includes 12 idioms, 6 similes, 6 metaphors, 6 examples of hyperbole and 6 examples of personification.

The initial game asks what the language means **in the supplied context**. The deliberately silly literal caption is a springboard for comparison, not a definition of the expression. Read the whole short passage before choosing. Each item has one intended meaning in its passage; open-ended interpretations of students' own writing can have several defensible answers.

- An idiom is an established expression whose customary meaning cannot be worked out simply by reading every word literally.
- A simile makes an explicit comparison, often using *like* or *as*. Not every sentence containing *like* or *as* is a simile.
- A metaphor describes something in terms of something else through a direct comparison.
- Hyperbole deliberately exaggerates for effect. It is not a measurement or a factual claim to memorize.
- Personification gives a nonhuman thing human characteristics, intentions, feelings or actions.

These labels are useful lenses, not perfectly separate boxes. An idiom can also contain a metaphor; a metaphor can personify. The bank assigns one main teaching focus to each item. Discuss another label when students support it with the wording. Avoid marking a thoughtful alternative wrong simply because a single category is stored in the game data.

The literal pictures are playful exaggerations. They do not explain an idiom's historical origin. In particular, *once in a blue moon* means very rarely; the game does not use it to calculate lunar dates. *A ton*, *a million years*, *a thousand times*, and *a river* are deliberate exaggerations in their respective passages.

Try these open-ended extensions:

- Give the same expression two short contexts: one literal and one figurative. Ask a partner which clues changed the meaning.
- Replace a figurative phrase with a plain statement. What feeling or emphasis was lost?
- Write two different captions for the same animated object. Explain the intended meaning of each.
- Make a metaphor that is helpful, then one that is confusing. Compare the features being connected.
- Choose a wrong answer and revise the passage until that interpretation would make sense.

Allow students to draw, speak, dictate or write their explanation. Familiarity with English idioms varies with language and experience. Read-aloud supports access but does not replace discussing unfamiliar expressions.

Implementation notes: `choices[0]` is the intended response before display shuffling. `evidence` explains the original `context`. The optional `alternateContext` has the same idiomatic meaning but should be paired with a new explanation if used as a separate assessed question. `literalCaption` is intentionally fanciful and may go beyond the available object sprite; display it as an imaginative caption rather than claiming the sprite depicts every detail.
