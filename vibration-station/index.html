CLASSROOM TEACHER NOTES

Vibration Station

Curriculum: Alberta Grade 2 Science, light and sound. The curriculum identifies sound as being produced by vibrations, and explores pitch (high/low), volume (loud/quiet) and duration (short/long).

Three investigations: Strings explore how vibration frequency relates to pitch; drum strikes change amplitude and perceived loudness; Sound Hunt contrasts sources and short/long durations.

Try these prompts

  • What object is vibrating to make each sound?
  • How does tightening a string change its vibration and pitch?
  • What happens to the waveform when you hit the drum harder?
  • Which sounds end quickly? Which last longer?

Three different representations: The string panel shows transverse displacement of an ideal string with fixed ends. The air panel shows a one-dimensional slice of longitudinal sound: particles oscillate around their own positions while compressions travel outward. The optional sound monitor graphs generated digital audio samples against time. It does not measure actual air pressure; no microphone is used.

One slow-motion scale: Both motion diagrams run 220 times slower than their model frequencies. A string tuned to 220, 440, or 880 Hz therefore appears as 1, 2, or 4 cycles per second. Matching strings at matching tensions have matching motion. The diagrams replay the first 0.036 seconds for eight seconds, while the audio plays at normal speed and fades sooner. The replay can continue after the audible note stops.

Fair string comparisons: The three ideal strings have the same length, material, thickness, and mass per unit length. They all begin at the same tension and pitch (440 Hz). Each string keeps its own tension setting, so tuning one does not change the others. They use the same sine-wave tone and audio envelope, so pitch is not confused with a different instrument sound. At the same tension all three sound alike. At the loosest slider setting any string is 220 Hz; at the starting setting it is 440 Hz; at the tightest it is 880 Hz. Frequency follows f = √(T/μ)/(2L); the tension slider changes only T in this ideal model. Lower/same/higher compares each string with its 440 Hz starting pitch. Colour identifies a string; it does not cause pitch.

Limits of the model: The instrument artwork is illustrative; its pictured bands were not measured. Real rubber bands can also change length, mass per unit length, and elasticity when stretched. Real plucked strings include harmonics; this model isolates their fundamental. An instrument's body or soundboard helps transfer string vibration to the air. The enlarged air-particle motion is schematic and not to scale. Sound speed is held at 343 m/s for all pitches in the same model air; higher frequency gives shorter wavelength, not faster sound.

Volume and duration: Drum strength changes signal amplitude while preserving the same model pitch pattern and decay. Device volume and hearing also affect perceived loudness. Bell, bee, and clap are synthesized examples, not recordings or rules that every such sound must be long or short. Muting the device does not change the model's vibration or signal. Start with low device volume.

Science references: OpenStax: sound and longitudinal waves; UNSW: strings, standing waves, and harmonics.

Privacy and accessibility: No recordings, microphone, login or analytics. Keyboard-accessible choices and visible text feedback work even when device audio is muted.
